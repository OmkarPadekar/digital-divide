Theory & context

The divide is not a gap. It is a chain.

A person can live under a strong signal and still be excluded from the benefits of being online. The theory behind this framework follows the chain from availability to agency and impact.

The central idea

Digital inclusion is a journey, not a yes-or-no status.

A household is digitally included only when a connection can be reached, afforded, understood and translated into something useful. Each condition depends on the one before it, but progress in one layer does not automatically create progress in the next.

That is why a high coverage rate can exist alongside low use of online public services, weak digital safety or limited economic benefit. Measuring the full chain helps reveal where participation is being interrupted.

01
Infrastructure · access

A connection must exist before it can be used.

Infrastructure is the physical and technical foundation of inclusion: fixed or mobile networks, electricity, a suitable device and enough reliability to complete everyday tasks. A signal that disappears, a shared phone with limited storage or a connection that is too slow for a video lesson can all create barriers even when a household is technically “covered”.

This layer is about more than geographic coverage. It asks whether people can connect at the time, speed and quality their lives require. It also includes the practical conditions around access: device ownership, repair options, accessible design and the availability of places where people can connect safely.

Key questionCan people reach a reliable connection with a usable device?
02
Digital literacy · capability

Access becomes meaningful through confidence and skill.

Digital literacy describes the ability to do more than open an app. It includes finding and judging information, communicating, creating, solving problems, protecting personal data and recognizing scams or manipulation. These skills shape whether a person can act independently rather than relying on someone else to complete every task.

Capability is shaped by age, language, education, disability, prior experience and the quality of support available. It grows through practice and trustworthy guidance. A service that is technically online can still be inaccessible if its instructions, language or interface assumes knowledge that users do not have.

Key questionCan people use digital tools safely, confidently and independently?
03
Affordability · stability

One successful connection is not the same as sustainable access.

Affordability asks whether a household can keep using a connection after the first sign-up. The relevant cost includes data or broadband plans, device payments, repairs, electricity and the opportunity cost of time spent getting online. A service can be available and technically affordable while still forcing people to ration data or choose between connectivity and other essentials.

Stability matters because interrupted access breaks continuity. Missing a deadline, losing a phone number or being unable to update software can make digital services unreliable for the people who need them most. Affordability is therefore a measure of resilience, not just price.

Key questionCan people maintain access without sacrificing basic needs?
04
Socio-economic outcomes · impact

The final test is whether access changes what people can do.

Outcomes are the benefits that make digital inclusion worth pursuing: better access to education, work, health information, financial tools, public services, community and voice. They are not guaranteed by the presence of a network. A person may be connected but still excluded from a job application because the process is hard to navigate, from a health service because it is not accessible, or from learning because there is no quiet place to study.

This layer keeps measurement focused on human results rather than infrastructure alone. It also reminds us that responsibility is shared: services need to be designed for real users, institutions need to build trust, and policy needs to remove structural barriers.

Key questionDoes digital participation create a measurable improvement in everyday life?
Putting the theory to work

Measure the point where progress gets stuck.

The layered score is not intended to reduce a complex social issue to one perfect number. It is a practical lens for comparing profiles, finding the weakest layer and choosing a more targeted response.
